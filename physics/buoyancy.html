Buoyancy and composition ​

The body force in the momentum equation is radial, b=br^, built each step by compute_buoyancy (physics/compute_buoyancy.jl) from a density perturbation:

b=−gδρ,δρ=δρT+δρphase+δρClap+δρC,

positive outward, so light material rises. Gravity g = cfg.g is uniform in radius. Every thermal and phase term is a lateral anomaly: its horizontal mean at each radius is removed, because a purely radial body force is balanced by a pressure gradient and drives no flow. The hydrostatic reference therefore never enters the solve.

The force is built one density contribution at a time and added up at the end. Temperature comes first, and a cell hotter than the rest of its row is lighter. Phase transitions come next, and they matter only where heat or cold has moved a boundary up or down. Composition follows. Depleted residue is lighter, while pile material and basalt that has turned to eclogite are heavier. The sum of these density differences, multiplied by gravity, is the push the flow solver sees.

The stages of compute_buoyancy (2-D) on a strip of eight columns, with Clapeyron buoyancy switched on (cfg.useClaperonBuoyancy = true) and cfg.Nr = 128. Two example cells sit in the first column; the other seven are the reference adiabat with no composition, so each lateral anomaly is taken against a row mean that includes the example cell (a 200 K excess becomes a 175 K anomaly). The plume cell (734 km, +200 K, pile fraction 0.2) is made heavier, not lighter, because the negative Clapeyron slope lifts the 660 km analogue in hot material. The cell is therefore already in the dense phase while its row is not, which adds +66.7 kg/m³ against a thermal −17.3 kg/m³, and the pile adds +8.8 kg/m³, giving +58.1 kg/m³ and b = −570 N/m³. The drip cell (147 km, −150 K, basalt 0.6, depletion 0.15) gets +13.0 kg/m³ from its temperature, +120.0 kg/m³ from eclogite and −25.0 kg/m³ from depletion, giving +108.0 kg/m³ and b = −1059 N/m³. The thermal values are the Boussinesq form that compute_buoyancy returns; under the default cfg.compressibilityMode = "ALA" the driver replaces that term with the reference-state form given below. Every value is checked against compute_buoyancy itself. Script: docs/scripts/fig_buoyancy_pipeline.jl.

Physical background ​

What drives the flow ​

Mantle convection is driven by density differences in a gravitational field. A parcel that is lighter than the material beside it at the same depth is pushed upward, a heavier one sinks, and the speed at which either moves is set by how strongly the surrounding rock resists being sheared. The mantle is so viscous that inertia plays no part. Its Prandtl number, the ratio of momentum diffusivity η/ρ to thermal diffusivity κ, is of order 1023 or larger (Schubert, Turcotte & Olson 2001). The momentum equation therefore reduces to an instantaneous balance between the body force, the viscous stresses and the pressure gradient (Stokes flow). Whatever density field exists at a given moment fixes the velocity field at that moment, and the body force b built on this page is the only source of motion in that balance. Every other piece of physics in MILEVA (heating, melting, rheology) changes the flow by changing either this density field or the viscosity that resists it.

A useful scale for the speed this produces is Stokes' result for a rigid sphere of radius a and density contrast Δρ moving through a fluid of viscosity η (Turcotte & Schubert 2014),

vStokes=29Δρga2η.

With Δρ=50 kg m−3, Venus gravity g=8.87 m s−2 (NASA Venus Fact Sheet), a=100 km and η=1021 Pa s this gives about 3 cm per year. The velocity grows with the square of the size of the anomaly and falls inversely with viscosity, which is why both the density contrast and the viscosity field must be right before a model velocity means anything.

(B) The same physics in MILEVA: a blob 500 K hotter than a 1600 K mantle (Gaussian, 150 km standard deviation, at mid-depth) gives a body force through compute_buoyancy, and one direct solve of assemble_stokes with η=1021 Pa s returns the radial velocity shown, rising through the blob and returning on both sides, with a peak speed of 33.7 cm/yr. Where density differences come from ​

Three processes change the density of mantle rock at a given depth.

  • Temperature. Rock expands when heated. The fractional density change per kelvin is the thermal expansivity α, about 3×10−5 K−1 in the upper mantle and decreasing with depth by a factor of several towards the core-mantle boundary (Schubert, Turcotte & Olson 2001). A plume 100 K hotter than its surroundings is therefore lighter by roughly 0.3 per cent, about 10 kg m−3. Heating from below and within, and cooling from above, continually create thermal anomalies, and this is what keeps convection going.

  • Composition. Rocks of different chemistry have different densities at the same pressure and temperature. Partial melting preferentially removes iron and the garnet-forming components into the melt, so the solid residue (harzburgite) is lighter than fertile peridotite. Jordan (1978) proposed that this compositional buoyancy offsets the negative thermal buoyancy of cold, depleted continental roots, so that they neither sink nor rise. The melt, once it has frozen to basalt and been carried below a few tens of kilometres, transforms to eclogite, which is denser than the surrounding peridotite through most of the upper mantle (Irifune & Ringwood 1993). Material of distinct bulk composition at the base of the mantle, the thermochemical piles, is usually taken to be denser than the mantle above it.

  • Phase changes. The minerals of the mantle transform to denser structures as pressure rises, producing density steps of several per cent near 410 and 660 km depth on Earth (Dziewonski & Anderson 1981). A step that sits at the same depth everywhere drives no flow. A step that a hot or cold anomaly has displaced up or down does, because over the displacement the anomaly is in a different phase from its surroundings (Christensen & Yuen 1985). The sign of this effect depends on the slope of the phase boundary in pressure and temperature and is set out on the Phase changes page.

Only lateral differences matter ​

A density that changes with radius only, the same at every point of a spherical shell, produces a body force b(r)r^ that is the gradient of a potential, ∇Φ with Φ(r)=∫bdr. A change in pressure balances it exactly and no motion results. This is the hydrostatic state, and it is why the absolute density of the mantle, rising from about 3400 to 5600 kg m−3 between the top and the bottom of Earth's mantle (Dziewonski & Anderson 1981), never needs to enter the momentum equation. What drives convection is the difference between a point and the average of its radius. MILEVA uses this freedom in two ways. The thermal, phase and pile terms are written as lateral anomalies from the outset, which keeps the numbers the solver handles small and centred on zero. The depletion, eclogite and crust terms are not mean-removed; their radial mean changes only the pressure and leaves the velocity unchanged.

(A) A fully depleted layer 200 km thick at every angle and (B) a lens whose thickness varies as 100+100cos⁡2θ km, both as body forces from compute_buoyancy. (C) Largest speed of a direct assemble_stokes solve with η=1021 Pa s. The uniform layer drives 4×10−13 cm/yr, round-off, while the lens drives 5.9 cm/yr. Removing the row mean from the lens's force changes the velocity by 1.4×10−12 cm/yr, so only the lateral part of a force moves the mantle. Default shell, 64 × 256 cells, Boussinesq. Measuring buoyancy without units ​

Three dimensionless numbers summarise how strongly each kind of density difference competes with the others.

numberdefinitionmeaning
thermal Rayleigh numberRa=ρ0gαΔTD3/(κη)vigour of thermal convection across a layer of depth D and temperature contrast ΔT; see Governing equations
buoyancy numberB=ΔρC/(ρ0αΔT)compositional density contrast measured against the thermal one
phase buoyancy parameterP=γΔρk/(αρ02gD)effect of a phase transition with Clapeyron slope γ and density jump Δρk, relative to thermal buoyancy (Christensen & Yuen 1985)

The buoyancy number decides whether a compositionally distinct layer stays where it is or is stirred into the convecting mantle; laboratory experiments show a transition from a stable stratified layer to one that rises and sinks in domes as B falls (Davaille 1999). A negative P belongs to an endothermic transition that resists flow across it, and the more negative it is, the closer the mantle comes to convecting in two separate layers (Christensen & Yuen 1985). With the default parameters of MILEVA (ρ0=3300 kg m−3, α=3×10−5 K−1, g=9.81 m s−2, D=2890 km, and the 660 km analogue at γ=−2.5 MPa K−1, Δρ=400 kg m−3), P≈−0.11 for the 660 km analogue and +0.05 for the 410 km one.

A simple way to compare the terms on this page is the temperature difference that would give the same density difference, Δρ/(ρ0α). With the defaults, ρ0α=0.099 kg m−3 K−1, so the 50 kg m−3 of a full depleted residue is worth about 500 K of heating and the 200 kg m−3 of eclogite about 2000 K of cooling. Compositional anomalies are rarely as concentrated as these end members, but where they are, they outweigh the thermal ones.

(B) The buoyancy number of eclogite (200 kg/m³) and of residue or pile material (50 kg/m³) against the temperature contrast; the dashed line is the default contrast T_cmb - T_surf = 3700 K, at which B is 0.55 and 0.14. Thermal buoyancy ​

Under cfg.compressibilityMode = "Boussinesq":

δρT=−ρ0α(T−⟨T⟩r),

with ⟨⋅⟩r the mean over all cells at radius r (over θ in 2-D, over θ and ϕ on the Yin patch in 3-D).

where ​

ρ0

= cfg.rho0 (default 3300 kg m−3) is the reference density,

  • α

    = cfg.alpha (default 3×10−5 K−1) is the thermal expansivity, constant in this mode,

  • T

    is the cell temperature, the grid field Tg, and

  • T−⟨T⟩r

    is its excess over the mean of its row.

The minus sign makes a hot cell light: δρT<0, so b=−gδρT>0 and the force points outward. With the defaults a 100 K excess gives δρT=−9.9 kg m−3 and b≈+97 N m−3. This is the Boussinesq approximation. Density differences are kept only where they are multiplied by gravity, and everywhere else the fluid is treated as having the constant density ρ0 (Schubert, Turcotte & Olson 2001).

Under ALA and TALA the driver (main_SphCon.jl, step 4) replaces this term with the reference-state form

bT=ρref(r)α(r)g[(T−Tad)−⟨T−Tad⟩r],

using ρref, α(r) and Tad from compressibility (physics/compressibility.jl), and keeps the phase and compositional terms of compute_buoyancy. Since Tad depends on radius only, this is the lateral temperature anomaly weighted by the local density and expansivity.

The anelastic liquid approximation (Jarvis & McKenzie 1980) lets the reference density and expansivity vary with depth along an adiabat while still filtering out sound waves. For buoyancy its main consequence is physical rather than formal. A given temperature anomaly is less buoyant at depth, because α(r) falls towards the core-mantle boundary, and slightly more buoyant per unit volume where ρref is higher. The driver forms this term first, adds the output of compute_buoyancy, and subtracts that function's own Boussinesq thermal term, so the phase and compositional terms are shared by every mode and only the thermal term changes. The reference profiles and their parameters are described on the Governing equations page.

(B) One row at 300 km with a broad 200 K warm region. The row mean is 20 K above the background, so the anomaly peaks at 180 K and the rest of the row is 20 K cold; the dotted curve is the thermal density of compute_buoyancy on the right-hand axis, flipped so that it overlies the anomaly. Phase transitions ​

The 410, 520 and 660 km analogues and post-perovskite enter as smooth phase functions Γk of pressure and temperature with their horizontal mean removed, δρphase+δρClap=∑kΔρk(Γk−⟨Γk⟩r), so they drive flow only through the temperature deflection of each boundary. cfg.useClaperonBuoyancy switches on the 410 and 660 analogues. cfg.usePhaseTransitionDensity adds the 520 km term (and post-perovskite with cfg.usePostPerovskite), plus the 410 and 660 terms when Clapeyron buoyancy is off; it acts only when cfg.useMeltingPhase is on, so a default run has no phase buoyancy. Each step has its own width, derived from the mesh at its own depth. Slopes, depths, density jumps, the mesh-derived width and the latent heat of the transitions are on the Phase changes page.

In words: Γk runs from 0 above transition k to 1 below it, and Δρk is the density jump across it. Subtracting the row mean ⟨Γk⟩r leaves a non-zero value only in cells whose phase differs from the average of their radius, which happens where temperature has moved the boundary. An exothermic transition (positive Clapeyron slope, the 410 and 520 km analogues) is crossed early by cold material and late by hot material, so it adds to the thermal buoyancy and helps flow through it. An endothermic one (negative slope, the 660 km analogue) does the reverse and opposes the thermal buoyancy, which is why the plume cell in the figure above ends up heavier than its row. The real transitions are only a few kilometres wide, much thinner than a convection cell, so the tanh width is a numerical choice tied to the mesh.

(A) Lateral temperature anomaly of a column 300 K hotter (left) and one 300 K colder (right) than the 1600 K adiabat below 200 km. (B) The phase part of compute_buoyancy (Clapeyron 410 and 660 terms plus the 520 term, phase field passed). The hot column is lighter at the exothermic 410 and 520 km analogues and denser at the endothermic 660 km analogue, and the cold column the reverse. Dotted lines mark the default depths 453, 520 and 730 km. Default shell, ALA pressure, 128 × 512 cells. Script: docs/scripts/fig_buoyancy_sections.jl.

Compositional buoyancy ​

Composition lives on the markers and is projected to cells as weighted cell means (basalt_evolution! when melting is on, project_marker_field_2d otherwise). Four fields enter δρC:

δρC=−Δρdepmin(CdepFmax,1)+Δρpile(Cpile−⟨Cpile⟩r)+ΔρeclCbasfecl(z,T)−ΔρcrustXcrust.

where ​

Cdep

is the depletion, the fraction of the parcel already extracted as melt, and Fmax = cfg.fertileFraction is the largest fraction that can be extracted, so min(Cdep/Fmax,1) runs from 0 for fertile mantle to 1 for a fully depleted residue;

  • Cpile

    is the fraction of thermochemical-pile material in the cell;

  • Cbas

    is the basalt fraction and fecl(z,T) the share of that basalt that has turned to eclogite at depth z;

  • Xcrust

    is the crust fraction defined below; and

  • the four Δρ are the density contrasts of the table, all positive, so the signs in the equation say which materials are light (residue, crust) and which are heavy (pile, eclogite).

Each term is linear in its composition field, so a cell half full of eclogite carries half the eclogite contrast. This represents each cell as a mixture of two end members whose densities differ by a fixed amount, and it lets the compositional fields be projected from the markers as simple cell means. The depletion term is capped at one, so a value of Cdep above Fmax adds no further deficit.

termparameterdefaultmeaning
depleted residuecfg.depletionDensityAnomaly50 kg m−3deficit of the full residue, reached at Cdep=Fmax = cfg.fertileFraction (0.30)
thermochemical pilemat.tcpile_drho50 kg m−3excess of pile material; lateral anomaly only
basalt / eclogitecfg.basaltDensityAnomaly200 kg m−3excess of eclogite below the transition
crustcfg.crustDensityAnomaly0deficit of crust relative to the mantle reference

Eclogite. fecl(z,T) is the eclogite fraction of basalt. It is zero above cfg.eclogiteTransitionDepth (60 km), rising to one over the 20 km below it, with an optional temperature-gated metastability (cfg.useEclogiteKinetics). Its definition is on the Phase changes page.

Depleted residue. Melting leaves behind a residue that is poorer in iron and in the components that form garnet, and therefore lighter than the fertile mantle it came from. The default deficit of 50 kg m−3 for a fully depleted residue follows Jordan (1978) and Schutt & Lesher (2006). The term matters most where the residue is also cold. In a thick, depleted lithospheric root the compositional lightness works against the thermal heaviness, and the balance between the two decides whether the root is stable, sinks or spreads. Because the deficit is applied without removing a row mean, a row that is uniformly depleted feels only a change in pressure, and a depleted region drives flow only where it borders fertile mantle at the same depth.

Basalt and eclogite. Basalt produced by melting is lighter than the mantle while it stays at low pressure. Carried deeper, as part of a thickening crust, a delaminating lower crust or a cold drip, it transforms to eclogite and becomes one of the densest materials in the upper mantle (Irifune & Ringwood 1993). The transformation therefore turns a buoyant layer into a heavy one over a short depth interval, and is a common driver of lithospheric drips in models of planets without plate tectonics. MILEVA represents this with the depth ramp fecl(z) and, optionally, with a temperature gate that keeps cold eclogite dense when it is carried back above the transition depth.

Half of the annulus with (A) a fully depleted keel 250 km thick, (B) a 30 km basaltic crust of fraction 0.5 and a drip of fraction 0.6 to 450 km depth, and (C) a 250 km high pile at the base. (D) The compositional density δρC of compute_buoyancy: the keel is 50 kg/m³ light, the crust is neutral (the default crust contrast is zero), the drip is 120 kg/m³ heavy below the eclogite ramp, and the pile is heavy inside and slightly light outside, because only its lateral anomaly enters. Crust as a material ​

The crust fraction (crust_fraction in physics/compute_buoyancy.jl) is the painted crust (the marker field C, projected as :continent) plus the basalt that sits above the eclogite transition, using the same 20 km ramp fecl(z):

Xcrust=clamp(Ccont+Cbas[1−fecl(z)], 0, 1).

It lowers the density by cfg.crustDensityAnomaly (for example 3300−2800=500 kg m−3) and, on the simple viscosity path, scales the viscosity by cfg.crustViscosityFactor (see Rheology). Both default to off.

Shallow basalt is neutral by default

With crustDensityAnomaly = 0 (the default) basalt shallower than the eclogite transition carries no density contrast; set this parameter to give the crust its buoyancy. The crust term exists in the 2-D buoyancy only; the 3-D dispatch has the depletion, pile and eclogite terms but no crust term.

(B) δρC of the basaltic column from compute_buoyancy: with the default crustDensityAnomaly = 0 the basalt is neutral above 60 km, and with 500 kg/m³ it is 500 kg/m³ light there; below 80 km both carry the eclogite excess. 600 radial cells. Thermochemical piles ​

cfg.useThermochemicalPiles (default false) seeds dense basal material from cfg.tcPileInitTheta and tcPileHeightFrac and tracks it on markers (thermochemical_piles!, physics/thermochemical_piles.jl). Pile material is denser by mat.tcpile_drho, has its ductile viscosity scaled by mat.tcpile_eta_factor on the composite path, and carries cfg.radioHeatPileFactor times the mantle heat production. The fields cfg.tcPileDensityAnomaly and cfg.tcPileViscMult are not read by the buoyancy or the rheology; the live values are the mat.tcpile_* fields. If compute_buoyancy is called without a material table it uses the MaterialProperties defaults, so the pile excess is then the default mat.tcpile_drho.

Unlike the depletion and eclogite terms, the pile term is written as a lateral anomaly, Cpile−⟨Cpile⟩r. Piles are a model for the large, compositionally distinct regions seismology finds at the base of Earth's mantle. Whether such material stays as a basal layer, gathers into piles or is entrained by plumes depends on its buoyancy number B and on its viscosity relative to the mantle above it (Davaille 1999).

(B) Its value inside and outside the pile against the covered fraction f, which is Δρpile(1−f) and −Δρpilef because the row mean is removed. A pile that covered the whole base would drive no flow. Sticky air ​

cfg.useStickyAir overrides the top cfg.airThickness of cells with viscosity cfg.airViscosity and writes cfg.airDensity into the buoyancy field there (apply_sticky_air!, utils/sticky_air.jl). The value is written into an array of force densities, so with the default airDensity = 1.0 the air layer carries a buoyancy of 1 N m−3, effectively zero. See Free surface and isostasy.

Assumptions and simplifications ​

The buoyancy in MILEVA rests on the following choices. Each is standard in mantle-convection modelling and each can be relaxed only by changing the code.

  • Uniform, radial gravity. g = cfg.g is the same at every radius and points to the centre. In Earth's mantle gravity varies by about ten per cent between the surface and the core-mantle boundary (Dziewonski & Anderson 1981). The density anomalies do not modify the gravity field (no self-gravitation).

  • Additive contributions. The total anomaly is the sum of thermal, phase and compositional parts. Cross effects, such as a thermal expansivity that depends on composition or a basalt density contrast that depends on temperature, are not included.

  • Fixed compositional contrasts. Each compositional Δρ is a constant. In reality the contrast of basaltic material changes with depth through its own sequence of phase changes, and below the 660 km transition it is briefly lighter than the surrounding mantle (Irifune & Ringwood 1993). MILEVA keeps the eclogite contrast constant below the transition ramp.

  • Smooth phase transitions. Each phase change is a tanh step whose width is set by the mesh rather than by mineral physics, so that the force is resolved on the grid and continuous in temperature.

  • Boussinesq or anelastic thermal term. The thermal term uses a constant ρ0α under Boussinesq and the depth-dependent reference state under ALA and TALA; the phase and compositional terms are the same in all modes.

  • Lateral means over a patch. In 3-D the row mean is taken over the (θ,ϕ) cells of each Yin-Yang patch separately, not over the whole sphere.

Diagnostics ​

Setting _BUOY_PARTS[] = true makes the next compute_buoyancy call keep its thermal, phase, Clapeyron and compositional parts in _BUOY_LAST[], so a peaked buoyancy field can be attributed to the term responsible. It is off by default and costs nothing when off.

At 147 km the drip's eclogite dominates; at 734 km the Clapeyron term of the 660 km analogue outweighs the thermal term and reverses the sign of the total in both columns.

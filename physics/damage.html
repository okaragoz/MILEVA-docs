Damage, softening and healing ​

MILEVA has two independent ways of weakening material that has deformed:

mechanismstate variableweakenshealsswitch

Both default off.

Physical background ​

Why deformation history matters ​

A viscosity law of the kind described on the rheology page depends on the present state of a parcel, that is its temperature, pressure, composition and strain rate. Real rocks also remember how they have deformed. A shear zone that has accommodated large strain is weaker than the same rock next to it, because deformation has reduced its grain size, aligned its minerals, ground its fault surfaces smooth or let fluids and melt in. Once formed, such a zone stays weak for some time after the deformation that created it has stopped, and can be reactivated later.

This memory matters most at the top of a convecting planet. A cold lithosphere following an Arrhenius law is many orders of magnitude stiffer than the mantle beneath it, and on its own it forms a single immobile lid, the stagnant-lid regime that is thought to describe Venus at present (Solomatov & Moresi 1996). Earth's lithosphere instead is broken into plates that are wide and rigid in their interiors but separated by narrow, weak boundaries. Plate velocities are a few centimetres per year (Schubert, Turcotte & Olson 2001), and a plate boundary that takes 5 cm/yr of relative motion across a zone of order 50 km width shears at roughly 3×10−14 s−1 (velocity over width, so a strain-rate invariant of about half that), while the plate interior next to it barely deforms at all. Producing that contrast requires a rheology in which deformation makes rock weaker, so that a zone that starts to deform faster concentrates more deformation, a positive feedback called strain localisation (Bercovici, Ricard & Richards 2000). Plastic yielding of the lithosphere alone can break the lid (Moresi & Solomatov 1998), but the weak zones it creates vanish as soon as the stress drops. Weakening that depends on accumulated deformation, and recovers only slowly, lets weak zones persist and be inherited from one tectonic episode to the next (Bercovici & Ricard 2014).

Weakening mechanisms in rocks ​

Several microphysical processes weaken deforming rock, and they act in different parts of the lithosphere.

  • Loss of cohesion and friction in the brittle regime. At shallow depth rock strength is limited by frictional sliding on fractures, described to first order by Byerlee's law, a shear stress of about 0.85σn for normal stresses below 200 MPa and of about 50 MPa+0.6σn above it (Byerlee 1978). As a fault accumulates offset its cohesion and effective friction can fall. Continuum models represent this as cohesion or friction that decreases with accumulated plastic strain (Lavier, Buck & Poliakov 2000; Huismans & Beaumont 2003).

  • Grain-size reduction in the ductile regime. Dynamic recrystallisation during dislocation creep reduces the grain size, which in turn speeds up grain-size-sensitive diffusion creep and lowers the viscosity. This is the basis of the grain-damage theory of Bercovici & Ricard (2012) and of the damage equation for grain size of Rozel, Ricard & Bercovici (2011). MILEVA models it explicitly on the grain size page.

  • Fabric and other effects. The development of a fabric, in which weak phases become aligned and interconnected, can weaken ductile shear zones (Montési 2013). Shear heating, fluids and melt act in the same direction.

Opposing all of these is healing. Grain growth, crack sealing and the re-equilibration of fabrics return the rock towards its undeformed strength. The balance between damage and healing decides whether weak zones are persistent features or short-lived ones. In convection models with damage the occurrence of plate-like behaviour depends on that balance, and a hotter surface, which speeds up healing, favours a stagnant lid (Landuyt & Bercovici 2009; Foley, Bercovici & Landuyt 2012). This is one proposed reason why Venus, with a surface several hundred kelvin hotter than Earth's, lacks plate tectonics.

How MILEVA represents them ​

MILEVA does not model these microphysical processes one by one in the two mechanisms on this page. Each is a phenomenological state variable that stands for their combined effect.

  • Grid damage is a single number D between 0 (intact) and 1 (fully damaged) per cell. It grows where the strain rate exceeds a threshold, heals exponentially with a fixed time constant, and multiplies the whole viscosity. It has the structure of the damage models used to generate plates in convection calculations (Bercovici & Ricard 2012; Foley, Bercovici & Landuyt 2012), but is driven by the strain rate rather than by the deformational work, and has no grain-size or pinning physics behind it.

  • Strain weakening is the brittle-regime softening of the continuum lithosphere models, in which the accumulated strain carried by each marker lowers the cohesion of the yield stress (Lavier, Buck & Poliakov 2000; Huismans & Beaumont 2003). Strain-dependent weakening has also been used in global convection models (Tackley 2000, part 2). It never heals.

Scales and dimensionless numbers ​

Three ratios control what grid damage does in a run.

  1. Damage number. The product Λ=kaccΔε˙τheal compares the rate of damage accumulation with the rate of healing. Its steady state is Dss=Λ/(1+Λ) (derived below). For Λ≪1 the field stays close to zero; for Λ≫1 it saturates at 1.

  2. Memory ratio. The healing time compared with the convective time scale, for example the time a parcel takes to cross the mantle, decides whether a weak zone outlives the flow that made it. With the default τheal≈100 Myr a zone abandoned by the flow stays weak for about that long.

  3. Threshold against the flow. The threshold ε˙thr sets which parts of the domain can accumulate damage at all. As an order-of-magnitude estimate, a mantle flowing at 1 cm/yr over a length of 1000 km deforms at about 3×10−16 s−1, so the default threshold of 10−17 s−1 lies below typical convective strain rates and above those of a rigid lid.

The strength of the weakening is set by wmax. A fully damaged cell is 1/(1−wmax) times weaker, 100 by default. A cold lid can be many orders of magnitude stiffer than the mantle below it, so whether that factor, together with yielding, is enough to mobilise the lid is an outcome of the calculation and not something the damage parameters decide alone.

A: the steady damage of evolve_damage! run to equilibrium (1000 Myr in 50 kyr steps) at strain rates from just above the threshold to 10−14 s−1 and three accumulation rates, against Dss=Λ/(1+Λ); all points collapse onto the one curve of Λ. B: the time scale on which D approaches Dss, against the strain rate. With the default 1014 it is seconds to hours at any strain rate above the threshold, far below a 50 kyr time step, which is why the default field acts as a switch. Grid damage ​

Every time step, each grid cell does a small piece of bookkeeping. It first asks how much faster it is deforming than a threshold strain rate. That excess drives damage up, while healing pulls damage back down in every cell, in proportion to the damage already there. Their difference is applied over one time step, the result is kept between 0 and 1, and the new damage then lowers the cell's viscosity before the flow is solved. The diagram follows one deforming cell and one quiet cell through these steps.

Right: a shear-zone cell (ε˙II=10−15 s−1, D=0.30, η=1023 Pa s) overshoots to D∗≈1011, is clamped to D=1 and drops to 1021 Pa s; a quiet cell below the threshold (10−18 s−1, D=0.60, η=1022 Pa s) only heals, to D=0.5997, and is weakened by a factor 0.406 to 4.1×1021 Pa s. The values are checked against evolve_damage! and apply_damage_to_viscosity!. Script: docs/scripts/fig_damage_pipeline.jl.

physics/damage.jl carries a scalar damage field D with the dimensions of the cell arrays, a phenomenological damage variable in the spirit of the damage-rheology models of Bercovici & Ricard (2012). It evolves as

dDdt=kaccmax(ε˙II−ε˙thr, 0)(1−D)−Dτheal,

accumulating where the strain-rate invariant exceeds the threshold and healing everywhere at rate 1/τheal. evolve_damage! takes one explicit Euler step per time step and clamps D to [0,1]. apply_damage_to_viscosity! then reduces the viscosity,

η←clamp[η(1−wmaxD), ηmin, ηmax],

so a fully damaged cell is 1/(1−wmax)=100 times weaker with the default wmax=0.99.

Each term has a simple reading. The source term is proportional to the excess strain rate, so damage grows faster in faster-deforming cells, and to 1−D, so it slows down as the cell approaches full damage and cannot exceed 1 in the continuous equation. The healing term is a linear relaxation towards the intact state with a single time constant, independent of temperature or strain rate. The viscosity factor 1−wmaxD is linear in D, so half-damaged rock is weakened by about a factor 2 with the default wmax, and almost all of the factor 100 is reached only as D approaches 1. The bounds ηmin and ηmax are the same as for the rest of the rheology, so the damaged viscosity stays inside them.

In the discrete update the source and sink are both evaluated with the damage at the start of the step, Dn+1=clamp(Dn+Δt[kaccΔε˙(1−Dn)−Dn/τheal], 0, 1), which is why a large accumulation rate overshoots far above 1 before the clamp, as in the figure above. While the time step is still zero, as on the very first step, the driver passes Δt=1010 s to evolve_damage! instead.

In the time loop. Damage is evolved and applied in step 2, after the viscosity law and the visco-elasto-plastic correction and before the Stokes solve, with the strain rate of the previous step. On the composite path the nonlinear iteration rebuilds the viscosity in every sweep and reapplies the damage factor to it, but does not evolve D again: damage advances once per time step. D is an Eulerian field: it stays at its grid position and is not carried with the flow. It is saved in checkpoints and restored on resume, and is not written to the VTK output.

Steady state and healing. Under a constant strain rate the damage approaches

Dss=kaccΔε˙τheal1+kaccΔε˙τheal,Δε˙=ε˙II−ε˙thr,

on the time scale 1/(kaccΔε˙+1/τheal), and once deformation stops it decays as e−t/τheal.

Default accumulation rate. kacc is a rate per unit strain. With the default 1014, kaccΔε˙Δt is far above one for any mantle time step, so D reaches 1 in the first step in which a cell exceeds the threshold and the field acts as a switch: a factor 1−wmax on the viscosity wherever ε˙II>ε˙thr, released over τheal after the deformation stops. Values of kacc of order 1 to 10 give the gradual accumulation of the loading figure below.

A worked example. Take a cell deforming steadily at ε˙II=10−15 s−1 with the default threshold and healing time, so Δε˙=9.9×10−16 s−1 and τheal=3.15×1015 s.

When the deformation stops, a fully damaged cell returns to D=e−1≈0.37 after one healing time, about 100 Myr, where it is still weaker than intact rock by a factor 1/(1−0.99×0.37)≈1.6. Damage falls below 0.01 only after ln⁡100≈4.6 healing times, about 460 Myr. A weak zone created by grid damage therefore outlives the flow that created it by a time comparable with a mantle overturn.

Strain weakening of the yield stress ​

With cfg.useFullPlasticity = true the driver adds ε˙IIΔt to every marker's eps_acc after each solve (step 8, interpolated from the grid). The increment is the total strain of the step, in every cell, not only where the yield branch is active, and eps_acc never decreases. Markers created by reseeding take the cell value. The field is projected to the cells and read by composite_rheology, which weakens the cohesion of the Drucker-Prager yield stress:

Ceff=C0[1−W(1−1fsw)],W=clamp(εacc−ε1ε2−ε1, 0, 1),τY=clamp(Ceff+μP, 0, τmax).

The cohesion falls linearly from C0 at ε1 to C0/fsw at ε2. The friction coefficient μ and the cap τmax are not weakened. With the default fsw = cfg.strainWeakeningFactor = 1 the strain is accumulated but the cohesion is unchanged. Strain weakening acts only on the composite path; the simple viscosity path and the plastic cap of visco-elasto-plasticity do not read eps_acc.

The yield stress enters the rheology as the yield viscosity ηY=τY/(2ε˙II), combined with the creep viscosity as described on the rheology page. Lowering the cohesion therefore lowers the stress at which a cell starts to yield, and the effect is largest near the surface, where μP is small and the cohesion carries most of the strength. At depth the frictional term dominates and cohesion loss changes the yield stress little. Because εacc is a strain, the weakening is set by how much a parcel has deformed and not by how fast. A parcel deforming at 10−14 s−1 reaches the default onset strain of 0.5 in about 1.6 Myr, one deforming at 10−16 s−1 in about 160 Myr. The default window of 0.5 to 1.5 is a strain of order one, the range over which continuum lithosphere models commonly let a fault lose its cohesion (Lavier, Buck & Poliakov 2000; Huismans & Beaumont 2003).

Assumptions and simplifications ​

The two mechanisms are deliberately simple, and their simplifications are worth knowing when interpreting a run.

  • Grid damage is driven by strain rate, not by work. Damage theories derive the damage rate from the fraction of the deformational work that goes into creating new grain boundaries or voids (Bercovici & Ricard 2012). MILEVA uses the excess strain rate, with no dependence on stress, temperature or grain size. Grain-size reduction as a physical process is covered separately by the grain size model.

  • Healing is linear with one time constant. In rocks healing by grain growth is strongly temperature dependent, so hot regions heal faster than cold ones (Landuyt & Bercovici 2009). τheal is the same in every cell.

  • Damage is Eulerian. D stays at its cell and is not advected, so a damaged zone that is carried away by the flow leaves its damage behind. This is a reasonable approximation where the deformation and the material stay together, as in a plate boundary fixed relative to the grid, and a poor one where damaged material travels far before it heals. The accumulated strain eps_acc, in contrast, lives on the markers and moves with the material.

  • The strain-rate history lags by one step. Damage is evolved once per step from the strain rate of the previous solve, so it does not take part in the nonlinear iteration of the current step.

  • Strain weakening counts all strain and never heals. eps_acc sums the total strain, including viscous creep outside yielding regions, so material that has flowed far in the ductile mantle arrives at the surface already weakened. Brittle softening laws in lithosphere models usually count plastic strain only. Without healing a weakened marker stays weak for the rest of the run.

Scope

Both mechanisms are phenomenological. Their parameters are not calibrated against laboratory data, so results that depend on them should be presented together with a sensitivity test over kacc, τheal, wmax or fsw.

2-D and 3-D ​

evolve_damage! and apply_damage_to_viscosity! act element-wise on arrays of any dimension, and the marker strain accumulation and its projection have 2-D and 3-D methods. Both mechanisms behave identically in the annulus and on the Yin-Yang grid.

evolve_damage! on a 16 × 64 array and on a 16 × 12 × 36 array holding the same strain rates, kacc=10, 10−15 s−1 for 100 Myr and then none. The update is element-wise, so a cell follows the same history to the last bit whatever the shape of the array. Script: docs/scripts/fig_damage_sections.jl.

Flow laws ​

MILEVA carries published laboratory flow laws for the mantle and the crust as named presets (physics/flow_laws.jl). Each law is stored exactly as its paper prints it: prefactor and its units, exponents, activation energy and volume, the test geometry and the citation. Every number was checked against the original table where it could be read (13 October 2026); where only a secondary compilation could be read, the table below says so.

Physical background ​

How hot rock flows ​

Over geological time the mantle and the deep crust behave as an extremely viscous fluid, although they remain crystalline solids. They deform by solid-state creep, the slow and thermally activated motion of defects in the crystal lattice. Which defect carries the deformation depends on temperature, pressure, stress and grain size, and each choice gives a different relation between stress and strain rate. That relation, measured on rock samples in the laboratory and written as an equation, is a flow law (Frost & Ashby 1982; Karato 2008). Four mechanisms matter for convection models, and MILEVA has presets for all four.

  • Diffusion creep. Atoms diffuse, through the grain interiors or along the grain boundaries, from boundaries under compression to boundaries under tension, so each grain slowly changes shape. The strain rate is proportional to the stress (n=1, a Newtonian fluid) and falls steeply with grain size, as d−2 when diffusion through the lattice controls the rate and as d−3 when diffusion along grain boundaries does. The Hirth & Kohlstedt (2003) olivine laws use m=3. Small grains and low stress favour it.

  • Dislocation creep. Line defects in the lattice glide on slip planes and climb past obstacles by diffusion. More stress produces more mobile dislocations as well as pushing each one harder, so the strain rate rises as a power of the stress, n≈3 to 3.5 for olivine, and does not depend on grain size. Large grains and high stress favour it, and because slip happens on particular crystal planes it aligns the grains, which is the origin of much of the seismic anisotropy of the upper mantle (Karato 2008).

  • Grain-boundary sliding. Grains slide past one another, and the misfits this creates are relieved by dislocation motion. In the dislocation-accommodated form of Hirth & Kohlstedt (2003) it combines a dislocation-like stress exponent (n=3.5) with a weaker grain-size dependence (m=2), and it matters near the boundary between the two mechanisms above.

  • Peierls (low-temperature) plasticity. At low temperature and high stress the resistance of the lattice itself to dislocation glide, the Peierls barrier, controls the rate. The applied stress lowers that barrier, so the stress appears inside the exponential and the strain rate rises far more steeply with stress than any power law allows. It sets the strength of the cold lithospheric mantle at stresses of hundreds of megapascals (Kohlstedt et al. 1995; Mei et al. 2010).

All four are thermally activated. The factor exp⁡[−(E+PV)/RT] is the probability that a defect gathers enough thermal energy to make its rate-controlling step. E is the height of that energy barrier and V, the activation volume, says how much pressure raises it. Because E/RT is large, about 28 for E=375 kJ/mol at 1600 K, modest changes of temperature change the strain rate, and hence the viscosity, by large factors.

The mechanisms operate side by side in the same rock, each straining the material at its own rate. At a given stress their strain rates add, so the fastest mechanism dominates and the rock is as weak as its weakest mechanism allows. A deformation-mechanism map (Frost & Ashby 1982) draws, in stress-grain-size or stress-temperature space, the fields in which each mechanism wins. Karato & Wu (1993) concluded that the Earth's upper mantle lies close to the boundary between diffusion and dislocation creep, so that both have to be carried in a model and grain size decides between them.

Why the flow law matters ​

The viscosity is the coefficient that turns buoyancy into flow in the Stokes equations (see Governing equations), so it sets the velocity of convection and, through the Rayleigh number, which is inversely proportional to it, the vigour of the whole calculation. Three properties of a flow law shape the dynamics.

  • Temperature dependence makes the cold top boundary layer orders of magnitude stiffer than the interior. When the viscosity contrast across it is large enough the layer stops taking part in the flow and becomes a stagnant lid, the regime of one-plate planets (Solomatov 1995).

  • Stress dependence (n>1) makes the rock shear thinning. At a fixed strain rate the viscosity varies as η∝ε˙II(1−n)/n, so where the flow is fast the rock is weak, which concentrates deformation into narrow zones.

  • Grain-size dependence couples the viscosity to the microstructure, which is why diffusion creep and grain-size evolution (Grain-size evolution) belong together.

Near the surface creep is not the whole story. The strength of the lithosphere at any depth is the smaller of its frictional strength, which grows with pressure, and its creep strength, which falls with temperature; plotted against depth this gives the familiar strength envelope (Kohlstedt et al. 1995; Bürgmann & Dresen 2008). In MILEVA the frictional part is the yield law of Rheology and the creep part is the flow laws of this page, with the Peierls law covering the cold, high-stress regime between them.

Dislocation creep looks less sensitive to temperature and pressure than diffusion creep in this table only because the viscosity is compared at a fixed strain rate. At fixed strain rate the Arrhenius factor enters the viscosity with the power 1/n; at fixed stress both mechanisms feel the full activation energy.

At 1600 K and 5 GPa these presets give 3.7×1018 Pa s for diffusion creep and 2.5×1020 Pa s for dislocation creep, and the two become equal at a grain size of about 4 mm. Below that size diffusion creep carries the deformation, above it dislocation creep does. A factor of ten in grain size, well within its uncertainty in the mantle, moves the diffusion-creep viscosity by three orders of magnitude. This is why convection models choose grain size, water content or prefactor so that the mantle viscosity agrees with what postglacial rebound and related observations infer for the Earth, of order 1021 Pa s (Turcotte & Schubert 2014), and why a published flow law fixes the shape of the viscosity field more firmly than its absolute level.

Earth and Venus ​

Two conditions separate the planets for the purposes of this page. The Earth's upper mantle contains water dissolved in olivine as hydrogen defects, which weakens both creep mechanisms; the wet laws of Hirth & Kohlstedt (2003) describe that through the water fugacity fH2O or the water content COH. Venus is generally modelled with dry rheologies, and Mackwell et al. (1998) measured dry diabase for that purpose, finding creep strengths significantly greater than earlier measurements on diabase under as-received or wet conditions. Venus also has a surface temperature of about 735 K (Seiff et al. 1985), so for the same geothermal gradient its crust is everywhere hotter than the Earth's, and creep takes over from friction at a shallower depth. The crustal presets below (diabase, anorthite, plagioclase, quartz) are there so that this part of a Venus or Earth model can be represented by a measured law instead of a viscosity factor.

Using them in a run ​

The laws are used on the composite path (cfg.useCompositeRheology = true). Each field takes a key from the tables below; :none keeps the reference-viscosity creep laws of Rheology:

julia
mat.flowlaw_diffusion         = :HK03_dry_olivine_diffusion
mat.flowlaw_dislocation       = :HK03_dry_olivine_dislocation
mat.flowlaw_grain_size        = 5e-3                 # m (default 1e-3), unless cfg.evolveGrainSize
mat.flowlaw_water             = 0.0                  # fH2O (MPa) or C_OH (H/10^6 Si)
mat.flowlaw_crust_dislocation = :MZK98_maryland_diabase   # where there is basalt
cfg.usePeierlsCreep = true                           # law: mat.flowlaw_peierls

  • The mantle laws replace ηdiff and ηdisl in the harmonic sum. Below cfg.depth660km they, the grain-boundary sliding law and the Peierls law are all multiplied by cfg.eta660kmFactor when cfg.use660kmJump is on. With cfg.useGBS and a dislocation preset selected, the grain-boundary sliding term becomes the Hirth & Kohlstedt (2003) dry law, on its T < 1250 °C or T > 1250 °C branch cell by cell.

  • The crustal laws replace the basalt factor: where the basalt fraction is Cb, log⁡η=(1−Cb)log⁡ηmantle+Cblog⁡ηcrust.

  • The Peierls law (mat.flowlaw_peierls, default :Mei10_olivine_peierls) joins the harmonic sum where T<1100 K.

  • Depletion stiffening is a property of the mantle fraction of a cell only: ηmantle above already carries the full depletion factor ξf, and the crustal law is never scaled by it. The cell's net stiffening is therefore ξf1−Cb, full in mantle and absent in pure crust. Without a crustal law the basalt factor b mixes the same way, η=ηduct[(1−Cb)ξf+Cbb]. Yielding, the viscosity bounds and the smoothing act on the result as before.

flow_law(:key) returns a preset, FLOW_LAWS holds them all, and flowlaw_viscosity(law, T, P, eII, d, W) evaluates one.

From a laboratory law to a viscosity ​

A laboratory flow law says how fast a rock sample shortens under a given stress, while the flow solver needs the opposite, a viscosity at the strain rate the flow already has. MILEVA therefore takes each law exactly as published, converts the cell's strain rate into the strain rate of the laboratory test, and asks which stress the law needs to produce it. For the power laws that question has a closed-form answer; for the Peierls law, whose stress dependence sits inside an exponential, the code finds it by bisection. The stress divided by twice the strain rate is the viscosity of that mechanism, and the mechanisms are then added in parallel, the weakest one carrying most of the deformation.

The steps for Hirth & Kohlstedt (2003) dry olivine diffusion and dislocation creep and the Mei et al. (2010) Peierls law (cfg.usePeierlsCreep = true), 1 mm grains, at a lithosphere cell (1000 K, 2 GPa, 10−15 s⁻¹) and a mantle cell (1600 K, 5 GPa, 10−15 s⁻¹). In the cold cell the Peierls law (290 MPa, 8.4×1022 Pa s) and dislocation creep share the deformation, giving 5.1×1022 Pa s; in the hot cell diffusion creep sets almost all of the 3.6×1018 Pa s and the Peierls law is not evaluated. The cells carry no composition, so the factors of the Rheology page that sit between steps 5 and 6 are 1. Both values are checked against flowlaw_viscosity and composite_rheology. Script: docs/scripts/fig_flow_laws_pipeline.jl.

Laboratory laws relate the differential stress σ to the axial strain rate ε˙ of an axial (triaxial) compression test. Three forms occur:

ε˙=Aσnd−mWrexp(−E+PVRT)(power law; σ in MPa, d in µm),ε˙=A(σμ)n(bd)mexp(−E+PVRT)(Karato & Wu 1993; μ = 80 GPa, b = 0.5 nm),ε˙=Aσnexp[−ERT(1−(σσP)p)q](Peierls; σ in MPa),

where W is the water measure the law was fitted to: the water fugacity fH2O in MPa, or the water content COH in H/10⁶ Si on the Paterson (1982) calibration. For incompressible axial flow the test quantities relate to the invariants MILEVA uses (ε˙II=ε˙:ε˙/2, η=τII/2ε˙II) by

σ=3τII,ε˙=23ε˙II,

so a law is turned into a viscosity by solving ε˙(3τII)=23ε˙II for τII: in closed form for the two power-law forms, by bisection for the Peierls form. For a Newtonian law this gives the Trouton viscosity σ/3ε˙. The melt factor exp⁡(αϕ) of Hirth & Kohlstedt is not applied.

Where the conversion factors come from ​

In an axial compression test the sample is squeezed along one axis by the differential stress σ and shortens at the rate ε˙, while the two other directions are free and, for incompressible flow, lengthen at half that rate each. The deviatoric stress and strain-rate tensors are then

τ=σdiag(23,−13,−13),ε˙=ε˙diag(1,−12,−12),

(signs taken with shortening positive), and their second invariants τII=τ:τ/2 and ε˙II=ε˙:ε˙/2 are σ/3 and 32ε˙, which is the pair of relations above. Writing the law in invariants makes it independent of the orientation of the flow, so a law measured in one geometry can be applied to the general deformation of a convecting mantle. Leaving the factors out, that is, putting τII for σ and ε˙II for ε˙, overestimates the viscosity by a factor of 1.5 for a Newtonian law and about 1.7 for n=3.5.

For the two power-law forms the strain rate can be written as ε˙=Kσn, where K collects everything except the stress (the code evaluates it as the law's strain rate at σ=1 Pa). The stress and viscosity of the mechanism then follow in closed form,

τII=13(2ε˙II3K)1/n,η=τII2ε˙II,

which for n>1 is the shear-thinning viscosity η∝ε˙II(1−n)/n of the background section. The Peierls form cannot be inverted this way, because the stress sits both in a power and inside the exponential, so flowlaw_viscosity bisects log⁡σ between 1 kPa (PEIERLS_SIGMA_MIN) and σP (60 halvings). If even σP does not reach the required strain rate, the stress is taken as σP and the returned viscosity is a lower bound; if 1 kPa already exceeds it, the stress is taken as 1 kPa and the returned viscosity is an upper bound. The second case occurs for the n=0 laws, which give the finite rate Ae−E/RT at vanishing stress, near the 1100 K cutoff at slow strain rates. Every such return is counted, peierls_bound_hits() gives the count, and the first one is reported with a warning.

Each mechanism's viscosity is evaluated at the cell's own strain rate, and composite_rheology adds them as ηduct=(1/ηdiff+1/ηdisl+1/ηGBS+1/ηP)−1, a mechanism that is switched off contributing 1/η=0.

Assumptions and simplifications ​

The laws are used as their papers print them; the choices MILEVA makes lie in how they are applied.

  • Steady-state, isotropic creep. Each law describes steady-state flow of a randomly oriented aggregate. Transient creep after a change of stress and the directional weakness of a deformed, aligned aggregate are not represented.

  • One law per mechanism for the whole mantle. The selected laws, measured on olivine or its crustal counterparts, are evaluated at every depth. Below cfg.depth660km the lower mantle differs from them only by cfg.eta660kmFactor (with cfg.use660kmJump) and through the pressure term PV; there is no separate law for lower-mantle minerals.

  • Mechanisms combined at a common strain rate. Strictly, parallel mechanisms share the stress and their strain rates add. The harmonic sum of viscosities, each evaluated at the cell's total strain rate, is exact for Newtonian laws and where one mechanism dominates, and otherwise underestimates the viscosity somewhat; for two identical laws with n=3.5 contributing equally the factor is 21−1/n≈1.6.

  • Water as a fixed parameter. The water measure is one constant for the mantle and one for the crust. It is not computed from pressure and temperature, not transported and not removed by melting. The Peierls law is evaluated dry (W=0), and the water-saturated Karato & Wu (1993) laws carry their water in the prefactor.

  • Grain size. It is 1 mm (mat.flowlaw_grain_size) unless cfg.evolveGrainSize supplies it from the markers.

  • Melt. The melt-fraction factor exp⁡(αϕ) of Hirth & Kohlstedt (2003) is not applied, so partial melt does not weaken the creep laws.

  • Peierls plasticity only where it can matter. It is evaluated only where T<1100 K; at higher temperature creep is weaker by many orders of magnitude.

  • Pressure. The activation-volume term uses the lithostatic pressure of mantle_pressure, not the dynamic pressure of the Stokes solution.

  • Crust. Where there is basalt the crustal law is blended with the mantle law in log⁡η by the basalt fraction, rather than treating the two as separate phases.

Extrapolation

Laboratory tests run at strain rates many orders of magnitude faster than mantle flow, and usually at higher stresses and smaller grain sizes (Karato & Wu 1993; Hirth & Kohlstedt 2003). Applying a law in a convection model extrapolates it along its fitted exponents, so the activation volume, the water content and the grain size, all of which carry wide ranges, control the absolute viscosity more than the choice between well-calibrated presets.

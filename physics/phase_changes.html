Phase changes ​

MILEVA describes the solid-state transitions of the olivine system (the 410, 520 and 660 km transitions and post-perovskite), the basalt-to-eclogite transition, and the latent heat of these transitions and of melting. None of them is a marker state. Each is evaluated on the grid every step from the lithostatic pressure and the local temperature:

whatwhere it actsfunction
integer phase index phase_idgate for the smooth phase buoyancy; latent-heat counter when the smooth latent heat is offmelt_phase (physics/melt_phase.jl)
smooth phase functions Γkdensity in the momentum equationcompute_buoyancy (physics/compute_buoyancy.jl)
smooth phase fractions and their material derivativelatent heat in the energy equationmelt_phase
eclogite fraction fecldensity of basalt below the transition depthcompute_buoyancy

The pressure throughout is the lithostatic mantle_pressure(z, cfg) (physics/melt_phase.jl), the hydrostat of the reference density of the selected compressibility mode (ρ0gz under Boussinesq). Transition pressures are evaluated with the same function at the transition depth, so a transition sits at its nominal depth on the reference temperature whatever the reference state.

Physical background ​

What a solid-state phase transition is ​

Mantle rock is a mixture of minerals, and each mineral is stable only over a limited range of pressure and temperature. When a parcel is carried deep enough, its atoms rearrange into a more compact crystal structure of the same chemical composition. This is a solid-state phase transition. Nothing melts, but the density jumps, heat is taken up or released, and the elastic and seismic properties change. The seismic discontinuities of Earth's mantle near 410 and 660 km depth are the clearest evidence for such transitions. Their depths, widths and density jumps fit transitions in the olivine component of a peridotite mantle (Ringwood 1991; Bina & Helffrich 1994). The Preliminary Reference Earth Model puts density jumps of about 180 kg m−3 at 400 km and about 390 kg m−3 at 670 km (Dziewonski & Anderson 1981), close to the defaults MILEVA uses.

Venus ​

The transitions are set by pressure, not by depth. A body with the mineralogy of Earth's mantle but a different gravity has the same transitions at different depths. Venus has a surface gravity of 8.87 m s−2 against Earth's 9.81, and a similar mantle density, so a given pressure lies deeper by roughly the ratio 9.81/8.87 = 1.106. This maps 410 km to 453 km and 660 km to 730 km, which are the default buoyancy depths in MILEVA. Whether the deepest Venus mantle reaches the post-perovskite field depends on its core size and interior temperature, both poorly known; post-perovskite is switched off by default (cfg.usePostPerovskite = false).

The pressure of 13.9 GPa found at 410 km on Earth is reached at 454 km on Venus, and 22.9 GPa from 660 km at 730 km, which are the default transition depths of 453 and 730 km. The Clapeyron slope and boundary topography ​

Along a phase boundary the two phases have equal Gibbs free energy. Differentiating that equality gives the Clausius-Clapeyron relation,

γ=dPdT=ΔsΔv=−LTΔv≃ρ2LTΔρ,Δv≃−Δρρ2,

where Δs and Δv are the changes of specific entropy and specific volume from the low-pressure to the high-pressure phase, Δρ>0 is the density increase, and L=−TΔs is the latent heat released per unit mass on the downward crossing, the sign convention the code's Lk use (Schubert, Turcotte & Olson 2001). The high-pressure phase is denser, so Δv<0 on the way down. If it also has lower entropy, heat is released (L>0, exothermic) and γ>0. If it has higher entropy, heat is absorbed (L<0, endothermic) and γ<0.

The slope decides how the boundary responds to a temperature anomaly. Inside a cold downwelling a boundary with γ>0 is reached at lower pressure, so it rises; one with γ<0 is reached at higher pressure, so it is pushed down. The vertical shift is δz≃γδT/(ρg), the formula in the next section. Seismology maps exactly this topography on the 410 and 660 km discontinuities, and their opposite signs are one of the strongest tests of the phase-change interpretation (Bina & Helffrich 1994).

The 410 and 520 km analogues move up in cold material and down in hot material, and the 660 km analogue does the opposite. How a phase boundary pushes on the flow ​

A boundary that is flat everywhere adds the same density at every point of a given radius. That changes the hydrostatic pressure but cannot drive flow, which responds only to lateral density differences (see Buoyancy and composition). A deflected boundary is different. In a cold slab sinking through the 410 km transition, the boundary has risen, so a slice of the slab is already in the dense phase where its surroundings are not. That extra mass pulls the slab down and adds to its negative thermal buoyancy. At 660 km the boundary inside the slab is pushed down, so the slab stays in the light phase while its surroundings at the same depth have already transformed. The slab is then buoyant relative to them and is held back. A hot plume rising from below is resisted in the same way. Inside it the 660 km boundary is raised, so the plume stays in the dense phase over a depth range where its surroundings are already in the light one. The endothermic transition is therefore a partial barrier to vertical flow, and the exothermic ones are accelerators.

The strength of the barrier is measured by the phase buoyancy parameter of Christensen & Yuen (1985). Compare the extra mass per unit area from the deflected boundary, Δρδz=ΔργδT/(ρg), with the thermal mass deficit of a column of the same temperature anomaly over the mantle depth h, ραδTh. Their ratio does not depend on δT:

P=γΔρρ2αgh.

The phase buoyancy parameter P is positive for an exothermic and negative for an endothermic transition. With the default MILEVA values (ρ0 = 3300 kg m−3, α=3×10−5 K−1, g = 9.81 m s−2, h = 2890 km) the 660 km analogue gives P≈−0.11 and the 410 km analogue P≈+0.05. Convection calculations find that a sufficiently negative P splits the mantle into separately convecting layers, and that intermediate values give intermittent layering, in which cold material accumulates above the 660 km boundary and then drains into the lower mantle in large avalanches (Machetel & Weber 1991; Tackley et al. 1993). The critical value depends on the Rayleigh number, the viscosity structure and the geometry (Christensen 1995; Faccenda & Dal Zilio 2017), which is why the 660 km slope is treated as a sensitivity parameter here.

Latent heat ​

The same entropy change that sets the Clapeyron slope releases or absorbs heat as material crosses the boundary. A parcel that transforms completely changes temperature by roughly L/cp if no heat is exchanged with its surroundings. Material sinking through the exothermic 410 km transition is warmed, which pushes its uplifted boundary back down and partly offsets the deflection. Material sinking through the endothermic 660 km transition is cooled, which reinforces the barrier. Melting is the same physics with a larger latent heat. Rising mantle that crosses its solidus cools as it melts, and melt that freezes heats its surroundings (Melting). Because L and γ are tied through Δv in the Clausius-Clapeyron relation, a transition with a steep slope and a large density jump also has a large latent heat.

L/cp with cp = CP_REF = 1250 J/(kg K). The transition values are measured from melt_phase by integrating its latent-heat rate over a column sinking through each boundary at 1 cm/yr, which recovers the 150, 100 and −250 kJ/kg fixed in the function. That is +120 K and +80 K of warming at the exothermic 410 and 520 km analogues, 200 K of cooling at the endothermic 660 km analogue. The melting bar is cfg.latentHeatMelt = 400 kJ/kg, 320 K of cooling for complete melting on the way up. Script: docs/scripts/fig_phase_changes_sections.jl.

Basalt to eclogite ​

Basaltic crust is not stable at depth either. With increasing pressure, plagioclase breaks down and garnet grows, and basalt or gabbro transforms through garnet granulite into eclogite, a garnet and clinopyroxene rock that is denser than the basalt it formed from and, through most of the upper mantle, denser than the surrounding peridotite (Ringwood & Green 1966). This is what lets oceanic crust sink with a subducting slab on Earth, and it is a candidate trigger for the delamination of thick crust on Venus, where there is no subduction to remove it. The reaction is slow in cold, dry rock, so basalt can survive metastably well below its equilibrium depth and eclogite can survive above it (Ahrens & Schubert 1975). MILEVA treats this transition as a function of depth by default and offers the second kind of metastability as an option, below.

Olivine-system transitions ​

A phase transition is evaluated in a fixed sequence of small steps. The code first finds the pressure at each cell from its depth, then moves the transition pressure up or down with the local temperature (the Clapeyron slope), and turns the distance from that boundary into a smooth fraction Γ of transformed material. Only the part of that fraction that differs from the horizontal average makes a density anomaly, and so a force on the flow. When material moves through the boundary, the same fraction, with the same depth, pivot and width, sets how much latent heat it takes up or gives off. Phase boundaries ​

A transition k is a straight line in pressure-temperature space through the reference point (Pk,Tk) with Clapeyron slope γk=dP/dT (Pa K−1):

Pb(T)=Pk+γk(T−Tk),Pk=Plitho(zk).

A positive slope (exothermic, 410 and 520 km) puts the boundary deeper in hot material and shallower in cold material; a negative slope (endothermic, 660 km) does the opposite. For a lateral anomaly δT the boundary moves by

δz≃γkδTρg,

about 15 km for γ=2.5 MPa K−1 and δT=200 K.

The dashed line is the code's adiabat at 1600 K potential temperature. Where it crosses a boundary line is where that transition sits in an adiabatic column. Default shell. Phase index ​

When cfg.useMeltingPhase = true, melt_phase assigns every cell an integer phase (1 olivine, 2 wadsleyite, 3 ringwoodite, 4 bridgmanite, 5 post-perovskite) by testing P>Pb(T) for each transition in turn. The depths, slopes and pivots are those of the phase buoyancy below (phase_transitions, from mat and, for post-perovskite, cfg), so the phase index, the phase buoyancy and the phase latent heat sit on one boundary:

Phase buoyancy ​

The density enters through smooth phase functions (Christensen & Yuen 1985),

Γk=12[1+tanh⁡(P−Pk−γk(T−Tk)δP)],δρphase=∑kΔρk(Γk−⟨Γk⟩r),

with ⟨⋅⟩r the horizontal mean at each radius. A laterally uniform pressure gives Γk no lateral anomaly, so a transition drives flow only through the temperature deflection of its boundary. Behind an exothermic boundary a cold downwelling transforms early and is pulled down; behind the endothermic 660 km boundary it transforms late and is held back.

The argument of the tanh is the pressure distance of the cell from its own deflected boundary, P−Pb(T), in units of δP. A cell exactly on its boundary has Γk=1/2; one more than about 2δP beyond it is fully transformed. Subtracting the horizontal mean removes the part of the jump that every column shares, which would only alter the hydrostatic pressure, and keeps the part that drives flow.

Basalt to eclogite ​

Basalt (the marker field basalt, projected to the cells) transforms to eclogite below cfg.eclogiteTransitionDepth (60 km) over a 20 km ramp:

fecl(z)=clamp(z−zecl20 km,0,1),δρecl=ΔρeclCbasfecl,

with Δρecl = cfg.basaltDensityAnomaly (200 kg m−3). The transition is a function of depth only, so it is instantaneous and reversible: basalt brought back above zecl is neutral again. With cfg.useEclogiteKinetics = true (default false), eclogite above zecl stays dense while it is cold and back-transforms smoothly around cfg.eclogiteBackT_K (1000 K) over cfg.eclogiteBackWidth_K (100 K):

fecl←min{1, fecl+1z<zecl12[1−tanh⁡(T−TbackWback)]}.

The same 20 km ramp defines the crust fraction used for crustal buoyancy and viscosity (Buoyancy and composition). The transition carries no latent heat. Its consequences for the melt cycle are on the Melting page.

Latent heat ​

Both latent-heat sources are volumetric rates added to the energy equation; melt_phase returns their sum when cfg.useMeltingPhase = true. They are evaluated from the temperature at the start of this step and of the previous one, and the driver rescales the rate by Δtprev/Δt so that the energy applied over the step equals the energy booked.

Melting ​

QL=−ρ0LDFDt=−ρ0L(∂F∂t+v⋅∇F),

with L = cfg.latentHeatMelt (4×105 J kg−1) and F the grid melt fraction of melt_phase (linear between the Hirschmann solidus and the Andrault liquidus, see Melting). Melting cools, freezing heats. ∂F/∂t differences the two start-of-step fields, and v⋅∇F is evaluated upwind; in a statistically steady state the Eulerian part alone is close to zero even where material melts on its way in. When the marker melting scheme owns the latent heat (cfg.trackBasalt and cfg.useHeatPipe, 2-D), it books −ρ0L per unit of extracted melt at the source (basalt_evolution!), and QL is zeroed above cfg.meltExtractMaxDepth_km so the two do not count the same melt.

Assumptions and simplifications ​

  • Univariant boundaries. Each transition is a single line in pressure-temperature space for one component. Real mantle transitions are divariant loops in a multicomponent rock, and near 660 km the garnet component transforms in parallel with ringwoodite. The tanh width stands for all of this, and it is set by the mesh rather than by the mineralogy.

  • Pressure is lithostatic. The boundaries are located on the reference hydrostat, so dynamic pressure does not move them. The built-in reference densities that set the hydrostat carry no phase jumps; a Perple_X reference table (cfg.referenceTable) supplies its own pressure profile.

  • Buoyancy only. The phase functions change the density in the momentum equation. They do not change the viscosity, thermal expansivity, heat capacity or conductivity. A viscosity increase into the lower mantle is a separate, depth-fixed option on the Rheology page (cfg.use660kmJump).

  • One description of each boundary. The buoyancy, the phase index and the latent heat read the same depths, slopes and pivots (phase_transitions), and the buoyancy and the latent heat the same mesh-derived width. A wide width on a coarse grid therefore spreads the latent heat as far as the buoyancy, and transitions closer together than their widths (the 453 and 520 km analogues) overlap in both. The one exception is the optional basalt shift of the 410 and 660 transition pressures (cfg.compClapeyron_dP_410, _660, zero by default), which moves the buoyancy's boundary only.

  • Latent heats are independent parameters. The values Lk are fixed in melt_phase and are not derived from γk and Δρk. With the default slopes and density jumps, ρ0 = 3300 kg m−3 and 1600 K, the Clausius-Clapeyron relation would give about 66, 59 and −147 kJ kg−1 for the 410, 520 and 660 km transitions, against the 150, 100 and −250 kJ kg−1 in the code.

  • Eclogite by depth. The basalt-to-eclogite transition depends on depth only, not on temperature or reaction time, apart from the optional cold metastable eclogite above the transition. Metastable basalt below it is not modelled.

(A) The latent heats fixed in melt_phase (measured as in the latent-heat figure above) against γkTΔρk/ρ02 at 1600 K from the default slopes and density jumps. The code values are larger by factors of 2.3, 1.7 and 1.7. (B) Γ of the 660 km analogue along the adiabat on the default shell at Nr = 64, 128 and 256. The 10-90 per cent width is three cells in each case, 135, 68 and 34 km, so the transition sharpens as the mesh is refined and is never as narrow as the few kilometres of the real transition. Tests ​
